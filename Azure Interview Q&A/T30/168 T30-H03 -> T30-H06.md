# **T30-H03. How would you balance reliability against cost?**

**Answer:**

I would start with the **business reliability requirement**, not with a specific Azure architecture. I would define the workload's availability SLO, RTO, RPO, critical user journeys, and the financial or regulatory impact of downtime. Then I would choose the **least expensive architecture that can credibly satisfy those requirements**.

For example, I wouldn't deploy an internal reporting application active-active across three Azure regions simply because multi-region sounds more reliable. If the business can tolerate a four-hour regional recovery, a single-region multi-zone architecture plus backups and tested DR may be much more appropriate.

On the other hand, if a payment platform loses significant revenue every minute it is unavailable and requires an RTO of five minutes, the extra cost of zone redundancy, a secondary region, replicated data, spare capacity, and automated failover might be justified.

So my decision is essentially:

```text
Business impact of failure
        ↓
SLO / RTO / RPO
        ↓
Required failure scenarios
        ↓
Reliability architecture options
        ↓
Cost of each option
        ↓
Choose the simplest design
that satisfies the requirement
```

Microsoft's current Well-Architected guidance explicitly treats reliability as a trade-off with cost: additional replicas, spare capacity, DR infrastructure, observability, recovery testing, and multi-region deployment all improve resilience but increase both infrastructure and operational cost. ([Microsoft Learn][1])

---

## **1. Quantify the business requirement first**

I would ask:

```text
How much downtime is acceptable?

What does one hour of downtime cost?

Is the service:
├── internal productivity?
├── customer-facing?
├── revenue-generating?
└── safety/regulatory critical?

What is the required:
├── SLO?
├── RTO?
└── RPO?
```

Consider two applications.

### Internal reporting portal

```text
Availability target = 99.5%
RTO = 4 hours
RPO = 1 hour
```

An outage is inconvenient but not catastrophic.

### Payment API

```text
Availability target = 99.99%
RTO = 5 minutes
RPO ≈ 0
```

Every minute of downtime can mean:

```text
lost transactions
lost revenue
customer impact
reputational impact
```

Those two workloads should not receive identical reliability architectures.

---

# **2. Compare the cost of reliability with the cost of failure**

A useful decision model is:

```text
Expected business loss from failure
             vs
Cost of preventing/recovering from failure
```

Suppose:

```text
Regional outage impact
≈ ₹20 lakh/hour

Expected recovery without DR
≈ 6 hours
```

Potential business exposure is significant.

A secondary-region architecture costing substantially less per year may be easy to justify.

But for:

```text
internal development dashboard

outage cost ≈ very low
```

a permanently hot secondary region may be waste.

Microsoft's current Cost Optimization guidance similarly recommends comparing the cost of disruption against the cost of preventing or recovering from it rather than simply choosing the cheapest infrastructure. ([Microsoft Learn][2])

---

# **3. Build reliability incrementally**

I would generally move through reliability levels rather than jump immediately to the most expensive design.

For example:

```text
Level 1
Single instance
       ↓

Level 2
Multiple instances
       ↓

Level 3
Multi-zone
       ↓

Level 4
Backup + tested regional DR
       ↓

Level 5
Warm secondary region
       ↓

Level 6
Hot active-passive
       ↓

Level 7
Active-active multi-region
```

Each level increases:

```text
resilience
+
cost
+
operational complexity
```

The business requirement tells me where to stop.

---

# **4. Remove single points of failure before buying extreme redundancy**

Suppose the application has:

```text
3 AKS nodes
across zones
```

but every application request depends on:

```text
one VM-hosted database
```

Then spending money on additional application replicas may not materially improve end-to-end reliability.

I would first map:

```text
Client
  ↓
Ingress
  ↓
Application
  ↓
Database
  ↓
Cache
  ↓
Messaging
  ↓
DNS
  ↓
External dependencies
```

and identify the biggest reliability bottleneck.

Reliability investment should target the **weakest important dependency**, not whichever Azure resource is easiest to duplicate.

---

# **5. Multi-zone is often a better first step than multi-region**

Suppose the production workload only needs to tolerate:

```text
datacenter / zone failure
```

Then:

```text
Region A
├── Zone 1
├── Zone 2
└── Zone 3
```

can provide substantial resilience without the complexity of:

```text
cross-region replication
global routing
regional failover
duplicate firewall/network stacks
regional data consistency
```

Multi-region should normally solve an actual **regional-failure requirement**, not serve as architecture decoration.

---

# **6. Reliability costs more than Azure resources**

A common mistake is calculating only:

```text
VM cost
database cost
network cost
```

High reliability also requires:

```text
Monitoring
       ↓
telemetry cost

DR
       ↓
backup + replication

Failover
       ↓
engineering + automation

Chaos / DR tests
       ↓
engineering effort

24×7 critical service
       ↓
on-call staffing
```

Microsoft's Well-Architected Framework explicitly notes that reliability investment includes observability, DR testing, operational processes, and personnel—not only extra compute. ([Microsoft Learn][1])

---

# **7. Don't eliminate all spare capacity in the name of cost**

Imagine:

```text
Normal traffic capacity required = 8 instances
Provisioned = 8 instances
```

Looks financially efficient.

Then one Availability Zone fails and two instances disappear:

```text
Remaining = 6
Required = 8
```

Now:

```text
reliability fails
```

Autoscaling may eventually add capacity, but scaling isn't instantaneous.

So I may intentionally maintain:

```text
headroom
```

for:

* sudden traffic spikes,
* node failures,
* zone failures,
* autoscaling delay.

Microsoft specifically notes that spare/overprovisioned capacity can be a deliberate reliability measure even though unused capacity conflicts with pure cost optimization. ([Microsoft Learn][1])

---

# **8. But avoid excessive overprovisioning**

The opposite extreme is also wrong.

Suppose normal load requires:

```text
4 nodes
```

and the worst credible failure requires:

```text
6
```

Running:

```text
30 nodes permanently
```

may provide no meaningful additional business value.

Instead I could use:

```text
baseline capacity
+
reasonable reliability buffer
+
autoscaling
```

with appropriate minimum and maximum values.

The objective isn't:

```text
maximum possible reliability
```

It is:

```text
required reliability
at justified cost
```

---

# **9. Choose DR architecture from RTO/RPO**

### Loose RTO/RPO

For:

```text
RTO = 8 hours
RPO = 4 hours
```

I might use:

```text
single-region production
+
geo-protected backups
+
IaC
+
tested regional restore
```

instead of always-on secondary compute.

---

### Moderate RTO

For:

```text
RTO = 30 minutes
```

maybe:

```text
warm standby
```

where:

```text
Region B
→ core infrastructure exists
→ reduced compute
→ data replicated
```

and capacity is increased during failover.

---

### Very tight RTO/RPO

For:

```text
RTO = 5 minutes
RPO ≈ 0
```

I may need:

```text
hot secondary region
or
active-active
+
continuous replication
+
automated traffic failover
```

which costs much more.

Microsoft's mission-critical guidance specifically warns against overengineering availability beyond actual business requirements because multi-region replication and duplication have significant financial and operational cost. ([Microsoft Learn][3])

---

# **10. Consider graceful degradation before duplicating everything**

Suppose:

```text
Product page
requires:
Product API
Recommendation API
Review API
```

Do all three really need:

```text
99.999%
```

reliability?

Maybe:

```text
Product API
→ critical

Recommendation API
→ optional
```

Then instead of making recommendations extremely expensive and globally redundant:

```text
Recommendation fails
        ↓
omit recommendations
        ↓
core shopping works
```

Graceful degradation can sometimes achieve the **business reliability goal more cheaply** than making every dependency equally resilient.

---

# **11. Use managed service capabilities before building custom HA**

Before building complex custom clustering, I would examine built-in Azure capabilities.

For example:

```text
zone-redundant service option
managed database HA
geo-replication
autoscaling
managed backup
```

may be cheaper and operationally safer than building the equivalent ourselves.

The cost comparison should include:

```text
engineering
maintenance
testing
incident ownership
```

not just Azure list price.

---

# **12. Measure the actual reliability gained**

Suppose we spend:

```text
+40% infrastructure cost
```

to introduce a second region.

I would want evidence that it improves:

```text
RTO
RPO
availability
failure tolerance
```

through DR testing.

Otherwise we may be paying for:

```text
architecture that exists
but cannot actually fail over
```

That's not reliability.

---

# **13. Watch for diminishing returns**

Reliability tends to become increasingly expensive.

Conceptually:

```text
99%
  ↓ inexpensive

99.9%
  ↓ moderate

99.99%
  ↓ substantially harder

99.999%
  ↓ potentially very expensive
```

Going from:

```text
99% → 99.9%
```

may require basic redundancy.

Going from:

```text
99.99% → 99.999%
```

may require eliminating rare common-mode failures, complex active-active data strategies, operational automation, and much deeper testing.

The last “nine” can be extremely expensive.

# **14. Production example**

Suppose I'm designing an online API.

Requirements:

```text
SLO = 99.95%
RTO = 30 minutes
RPO = 5 minutes
```

I might choose:

```text
Azure Front Door
       ↓
App/AKS across Availability Zones
       ↓
zone-redundant database
       ↓
cross-region data replication
       ↓
secondary region prepared
but not necessarily active-active
```

Why not active-active?

Because:

```text
required RTO = 30 minutes
```

may not justify paying for full duplicate production capacity continuously.

Instead:

```text
Multi-zone
→ handles common infrastructure failures automatically

Secondary region
→ handles rare regional disaster

Warm DR
→ satisfies recovery target more economically
```

---

# **15. Continuously revisit the trade-off**

Reliability requirements change.

An internal application may later become customer-facing.

Traffic may grow.

Revenue impact may increase.

Azure pricing/service capabilities may change.

So periodically review:

```text
Actual SLI
Error-budget consumption
Incident frequency
Incident cost
Azure spend
Growth
Business criticality
```

Then adjust architecture.

Cost optimization is a **continuous process**, not a one-time architecture meeting. Microsoft's Well-Architected guidance similarly recommends continuously reviewing workload investment rather than relying only on short-term cost-cutting decisions. ([Microsoft Learn][4])

---

### **Interview answer condensed**

> “I would start with the business SLO, RTO, RPO, and cost of downtime, then choose the least expensive architecture that can credibly meet those requirements. I normally improve reliability incrementally—remove single points of failure, add replicas, use Availability Zones, establish backup and DR, and only move to hot or active-active multi-region when regional failure requirements justify it. I would also consider graceful degradation, managed Azure HA capabilities, and reasonable spare capacity instead of making every dependency maximally redundant. Finally, I'd test failover and monitor actual SLI/error-budget results because paying for redundancy that doesn't meet the recovery objective isn't useful reliability.”

**Memory line:**
**Reliability should be sufficient for the business requirement, not maximized without regard to cost.**

**Interview trap:**
The cheapest architecture isn't cost-optimized if outages cost more than the reliability investment—but the most redundant architecture can also be wasteful if the business doesn't require it.

**Keywords:**
SLO, RTO, RPO, cost of downtime, redundancy, Availability Zones, multi-region, DR, spare capacity, graceful degradation, trade-off.

**Likely follow-ups:**

* When would you choose warm standby instead of active-active?
* How would you justify multi-region cost to a business stakeholder?
* Why might spare capacity be intentional rather than waste?
* How could graceful degradation reduce reliability cost?
* How would you prove that money spent on DR is actually providing value?

# **T30-H04. How would you establish SLIs and SLOs for an API?**

**Answer:**

I would start with the **user journeys**, not infrastructure metrics.

For an API, the most important user-facing SLIs are usually things such as **successful request rate and latency**, and sometimes correctness, freshness, or durability depending on the API.

For example, for a checkout API I might define:

```text
Availability SLI
=
successful valid checkout requests
----------------------------------
all valid checkout requests
```

and:

```text
Latency SLI
=
valid successful requests completed <= 500 ms
------------------------------------------------
all valid successful requests
```

Then I would agree on business-driven SLOs such as:

```text
Availability:
99.95% over rolling 30 days

Latency:
99% of successful requests <= 500 ms
over rolling 30 days
```

I would define exactly what counts as a valid request, what counts as success, exclusions, and the measurement source so nobody can reinterpret the SLO after an incident.

Then I would instrument the API with Application Insights/OpenTelemetry, calculate those SLIs in Azure Monitor, derive the error budget from the SLO, and alert on **fast and slow error-budget burn** rather than waiting until the SLO has already been missed.

Azure Monitor now has native SLI support for Service Groups, including SLO baselines, compliance periods, error-budget tracking, and fast/slow burn-rate alerts. ([Microsoft Learn][5])

---

## **1. Start from what users care about**

I would ask:

> What does “working” mean to the API consumer?

For a payments API, probably:

```text
Request accepted
       ↓
correct transaction result
       ↓
within acceptable latency
```

Not:

```text
Pod CPU < 70%
```

CPU is useful operational telemetry.

But users don't care directly whether:

```text
CPU = 45%
or
CPU = 75%
```

They care:

```text
Did my payment succeed?
Was the response fast enough?
```

---

# **2. Identify critical API journeys**

Don't blindly create one global SLO for every endpoint.

For example:

```text
/api/health
/api/catalog
/api/checkout
/api/payment
/api/admin/report
```

may have very different business criticality.

I might group:

```text
Tier 1
Checkout + Payment

Tier 2
Catalog

Tier 3
Internal reporting
```

Then define different reliability objectives.

Otherwise:

```text
millions of successful /health requests
```

could hide:

```text
checkout failures
```

inside an aggregate SLI.

---

# **3. Define the availability SLI precisely**

A typical request-based availability SLI:

```text
Good events
-----------
Valid events
```

Example:

```text
Good:
server successfully processes valid request

Valid:
requests that should actually be served
```

Now define failure.

For example:

```text
500
502
503
504
→ typically service failure

400
→ often malformed client request
→ possibly excluded

401/403
→ depends on whether rejection was expected

429
→ depends on service contract
```

The important thing is:

> **Define this before measuring.**

You shouldn't exclude a failure merely because it makes the SLI look bad.

---

# **4. Example availability formula**

Suppose:

```text
Valid requests = 10,000,000
Successful      = 9,997,000
```

Then:

```text
SLI
=
9,997,000 / 10,000,000

=
99.97%
```

If the SLO is:

```text
99.95%
```

the API currently satisfies it.

---

# **5. Define latency as a good-event ratio**

A good latency SLI can be:

```text
Requests <= 500 ms
------------------
valid requests
```

rather than simply:

```text
average latency
```

because averages hide tail latency.

Example:

```text
9,900 requests = 100 ms
100 requests   = 10 sec
```

The average might look acceptable while those 100 users have a terrible experience.

I may also track:

```text
p50
p95
p99
```

operationally, but the SLO itself should be unambiguous.

---

# **6. Consider correctness when relevant**

An API can return:

```text
HTTP 200
```

while producing incorrect results.

For something like:

```text
payment processing
inventory
account balance
```

correctness may need its own SLI.

For example:

```text
correctly processed transactions
-------------------------------
accepted valid transactions
```

Correctness is harder to measure than HTTP status, but it may be far more important.

---

# **7. Define the measurement window**

An SLO without a period is incomplete.

For example:

```text
99.95%
over rolling 30 days
```

versus:

```text
99.95%
per calendar month
```

can produce different operational behavior.

I normally prefer a clearly documented rolling or calendar compliance period depending on organizational practice.

---

# **8. Select the target from business requirements**

I would not choose:

```text
99.99%
```

because it sounds professional.

Ask:

```text
What do customers expect?
What SLA exists?
What do dependencies support?
What is the cost of extra reliability?
What reliability have we historically achieved?
```

Example:

```text
External SLA = 99.9%
```

The engineering SLO might be:

```text
99.95%
```

providing margin before contractual impact.

But setting:

```text
99.99999%
```

for an internal batch API may cause massive unnecessary engineering expense.

---

# **9. Check whether the dependency architecture can support the SLO**

Suppose required API dependencies theoretically provide:

```text
Ingress       = 99.99%
Application   = 99.95%
Database      = 99.95%
```

A target of:

```text
99.999%
```

is unlikely to be credible without significant additional redundancy and degradation design.

So SLO definition and architecture must agree.

---

# **10. Instrument the API**

I would include:

```text
OpenTelemetry / Application Insights
        ↓
Request telemetry
Dependency telemetry
Exceptions
Distributed traces
```

with context such as:

```text
endpoint
status code
duration
region
service version
trace ID
```

Azure Application Insights provides failure, performance, and end-to-end transaction diagnostics that can support investigation of API SLI degradation. ([Microsoft Learn][6])

---

# **11. Example KQL for availability**

Conceptually in Application Insights:

```kusto
requests
| where timestamp > ago(30d)
| where name startswith "POST /checkout"
| summarize
    Total = count(),
    Good = countif(success == true)
| extend AvailabilitySLI = 100.0 * Good / Total
```

This tells me:

```text
What percentage of measured
checkout requests succeeded?
```

In a real environment I would refine:

```text
client-error exclusions
synthetic traffic
health probes
specific endpoints
```

according to the documented SLI definition.

---

# **12. Example latency SLI**

```kusto
requests
| where timestamp > ago(30d)
| where name startswith "POST /checkout"
| where success == true
| summarize
    Total = count(),
    Good = countif(duration <= 500ms)
| extend LatencySLI = 100.0 * Good / Total
```

Conceptually:

```text
Good
=
successful checkout request <= 500 ms
```

This can be much more actionable than:

```text
average request duration
```

---

# **13. Calculate the error budget**

If:

```text
SLO = 99.95%
```

then:

```text
Error budget
=
100 - 99.95
=
0.05%
```

For:

```text
10,000,000 valid requests
```

allowed bad requests:

```text
10,000,000 × 0.0005
=
5,000
```

That gives teams a concrete reliability budget.

Azure Monitor's current SLI experience calculates remaining error budget from the selected baseline/SLO target and tracks burn rate over the compliance window. ([Microsoft Learn][5])

---

# **14. Alert on burn rate, not only SLO violation**

Suppose:

```text
Monthly error budget = 5,000 failures
```

and the API suddenly produces:

```text
1,000 failures in 5 minutes
```

Waiting until:

```text
all 5,000 are consumed
```

would be foolish.

Instead:

```text
Fast burn
→ severe immediate regression

Slow burn
→ persistent degradation
```

Azure Monitor's current SLI feature supports both fast- and slow-burn alerts as early indicators that the service is consuming error budget too quickly. ([Microsoft Learn][5])

---

# **15. Avoid infrastructure-only SLOs**

Weak:

```text
CPU < 80%
99.9% of month
```

What if:

```text
CPU = 20%
API returns 500
```

The infrastructure metric passes while users fail.

Infrastructure metrics should help diagnose **why** the API SLI is failing.

The SLI itself should normally measure the service outcome.

---

# **16. Avoid making the SLO dependent on telemetry you can't trust**

Suppose:

```text
Application crashes
       ↓
telemetry SDK stops sending
       ↓
no failed requests recorded
```

Your measurement can look artificially healthy.

For critical APIs I might combine:

```text
server-side request telemetry
+
external synthetic availability test
+
gateway/front-door metrics
```

so observability itself doesn't become the blind spot.

---

# **17. Review SLOs periodically**

If the API continuously achieves:

```text
99.999%
```

but the SLO is:

```text
99%
```

maybe the target is too weak.

Conversely:

```text
SLO = 99.999%
```

but the business only needs:

```text
99.9%
```

and the team spends huge amounts of time maintaining unnecessary resilience, the objective may be too strict.

Review:

```text
user expectations
incident history
cost
architecture
business criticality
```

periodically.

---

### **Interview answer condensed**

> “For an API I'd start with critical user journeys and define user-facing SLIs such as successful request rate and latency, rather than infrastructure metrics like CPU. I'd specify exactly what counts as a valid request, a good request, exclusions, latency threshold, and measurement window. For example, checkout might require 99.95% successful valid requests and 99% of successful requests under 500 ms over a rolling 30 days. I'd instrument it through Application Insights or OpenTelemetry, calculate the SLI from request telemetry, derive the error budget, and alert on fast and slow burn rates so we detect reliability risk before the SLO is actually violated.”

**Memory line:**
**SLI = what users actually experience; SLO = the target we commit engineering to achieve for that experience.**

**Interview trap:**
CPU, memory, and Pod health are usually **diagnostic metrics**, not good user-facing API SLIs.

**Keywords:**
SLI, SLO, good events, valid events, success rate, latency, Application Insights, OpenTelemetry, error budget, burn rate.

**Likely follow-ups:**

* Should HTTP 4xx responses count against an availability SLO?
* Why would you use request-based availability rather than host uptime?
* Why is average latency a weak SLI?
* How would you alert before the SLO has actually been breached?
* What would you do if the telemetry system itself becomes unavailable?

# **T30-H05. What should happen when a team's error budget is exhausted?**

**Answer:**

When a team's error budget is exhausted, I would **change the team's risk posture**.

I would not automatically stop every deployment, but I would pause or heavily gate **nonessential risky changes** and prioritize work that restores reliability: fixing recurring incidents, reducing failure modes, improving observability, eliminating problematic dependencies, improving rollback, and addressing whatever consumed the budget.

I would first identify **why** the budget was exhausted. If one bad release caused a large fast burn, the response is different from six weeks of small recurring failures. I would review incidents and burn-rate trends, agree on remediation with product stakeholders, and establish clear criteria for returning to normal release velocity.

Security fixes, emergency mitigations, and reliability improvements may still need to ship because freezing all change can itself increase risk.

So the policy is:

```text
Error budget exhausted
       ↓
Reduce reliability risk
       ↓
Stop/gate nonessential risky change
       ↓
Understand budget consumers
       ↓
Prioritize reliability work
       ↓
Restore healthy reliability trend
       ↓
Resume normal delivery
```

Azure Monitor's current SLI capability can track remaining error budget and burn rate, with fast- and slow-burn alerts to identify rapid or sustained consumption before the budget is fully gone. ([Microsoft Learn][5])

---

## **1. First confirm the budget really is exhausted**

Before changing delivery policy, verify:

```text
SLI calculation correct?
      ↓
Telemetry complete?
      ↓
Correct compliance window?
      ↓
Correct valid-event filtering?
      ↓
Actual error budget <= 0?
```

I don't want a telemetry bug to freeze production releases.

For example:

```text
synthetic health probes
mistakenly included as user failures
```

could distort the SLI.

But I also wouldn't manipulate exclusions simply to make the budget look healthy.

---

# **2. Understand how the budget was consumed**

There are two very different cases.

### Case A — Fast burn

```text
New deployment
      ↓
30% requests fail
      ↓
huge budget consumption
in 20 minutes
```

Likely response:

```text
rollback
stop rollout
incident response
fix release safety
```

---

### Case B — Slow burn

```text
0.2% excess failures
every day
for weeks
```

Possible causes:

```text
flaky dependency
capacity issue
intermittent timeout
network instability
bad retry policy
```

The response needs longer-term reliability engineering.

That's why burn rate matters in addition to simply knowing how much budget remains. Azure Monitor distinguishes fast-burn conditions from sustained slow-burn conditions for exactly this reason. ([Microsoft Learn][5])

---

# **3. Pause nonessential risky releases**

If the service has already missed its reliability objective:

```text
normal feature velocity
```

should no longer be the default priority.

I might establish:

```text
Feature deployment?
      ↓
Does it reduce reliability risk?
   /                \
 Yes                 No
 ↓                    ↓
Allow with controls   Hold / exceptional approval
```

Examples likely to continue:

```text
critical bug fix
rollback improvement
capacity fix
security patch
observability fix
dependency resilience
```

Examples likely to pause:

```text
large UI redesign
major architecture refactor
nonessential database migration
high-risk feature launch
```

---

# **4. Don't implement a blind “no changes whatsoever” rule**

This is an important SRE nuance.

Suppose the error budget is exhausted because:

```text
memory leak
```

and fixing it requires a deployment.

A complete freeze would prevent:

```text
the reliability fix itself
```

which is counterproductive.

Likewise:

```text
critical security vulnerability
```

may require immediate deployment.

So I would use:

```text
change freeze on discretionary risk
```

not:

```text
production becomes immutable
```

---

# **5. Identify the dominant reliability consumers**

I would analyze:

```text
Which incidents consumed budget?
Which endpoint?
Which dependency?
Which region?
Which deployment?
Which failure category?
```

Example:

```text
Error budget consumption
│
├── 55% bad deployment
├── 20% database timeouts
├── 15% payment provider
└── 10% miscellaneous
```

Now the team's highest-value work is obvious.

Fixing the deployment path may return more reliability than spending three weeks tuning a minor dependency.

---

# **6. Distinguish owned vs external failures**

Suppose 40% of the budget came from:

```text
third-party payment provider
```

It may still count against the customer-facing SLO because users don't care whose fault it was.

But engineering response could include:

```text
secondary provider
circuit breaker
timeout
queueing
graceful degradation
contract/SLA review
```

rather than modifying internal database code.

The SLO should represent user experience; ownership determines the remediation strategy.

---

# **7. Review release/change correlation**

Ask:

```text
Did reliability degrade after deployments?
```

If yes, inspect:

```text
change failure rate
canary coverage
automated rollback
health checks
test quality
configuration validation
```

For example:

```text
Feature deployment
      ↓
5xx spike
      ↓
40 minutes before rollback
```

Reliability improvements might be:

```text
canary
+
SLO-based rollout gate
+
automatic rollback
```

rather than simply telling developers:

> “Be more careful.”

---

# **8. Reliability work should be concrete**

Weak reaction:

```text
"We should improve reliability."
```

Better:

```text
1. Fix checkout connection-pool exhaustion
2. Add circuit breaker for payment dependency
3. Add fast-burn alert
4. Implement automatic rollback
5. Increase minimum healthy AKS replicas
6. Load-test peak traffic
```

Each item should have:

```text
owner
priority
deadline
expected reliability outcome
```

# **9. Use error-budget policy agreed in advance**

The worst time to invent error-budget policy is during an outage.

I would define something like:

```text
Budget healthy
→ normal release cadence

Fast burn detected
→ incident / halt current rollout

Budget < 25%
→ additional review for high-risk changes

Budget exhausted
→ discretionary feature changes paused

Reliability trend recovered
→ normal delivery resumes
```

The exact thresholds should be agreed by:

```text
SRE / platform
+
engineering
+
product/business
```

because reliability and delivery are business trade-offs.

---

# **10. Product stakeholders need to be involved**

Suppose the service missed its SLO but product wants:

```text
major feature launch tomorrow
```

The discussion should be measurable:

```text
Current reliability
Error budget = exhausted
Recent incidents
Risk of another outage
Revenue/value of launch
```

Then stakeholders can make an informed risk decision.

Error budgets create a shared language between:

```text
feature delivery
and
service reliability
```

instead of making it an SRE-versus-developer argument.

---

# **11. Do a post-incident/reliability review**

If one or more significant incidents consumed the budget:

```text
Incident timeline
      ↓
Contributing factors
      ↓
Why detection was slow?
      ↓
Why blast radius was large?
      ↓
Why recovery took N minutes?
      ↓
Preventive actions
```

This is where blameless postmortems connect directly to SLO management.

---

# **12. Improve the highest-impact reliability mechanisms**

Possible remediation:

```text
Recurring deployment failures
→ canary + automated rollback

Dependency overload
→ circuit breaker + retry budget

Capacity exhaustion
→ autoscaling + headroom + load test

Region dependency
→ DR / redundancy

Slow incident diagnosis
→ observability improvement

Manual recovery
→ tested runbook / automation
```

The response should be driven by actual budget consumption, not random “reliability projects.”

---

# **13. Don't lower the SLO simply because you failed it**

Suppose:

```text
SLO = 99.95%

Actual = 99.70%
```

An unhealthy response is:

```text
Change SLO to 99.5%
      ↓
"Problem solved"
```

You may eventually discover the SLO is genuinely unrealistic and renegotiate it, but that should happen through:

```text
business requirement review
+
cost/reliability analysis
```

not to hide poor performance.

---

# **14. Don't reset the budget informally**

Similarly:

```text
Budget exhausted on day 12
      ↓
"Let's restart the 30-day window"
```

destroys the value of the process.

Compliance windows and SLI definitions should be stable and agreed in advance.

---

# **15. Recovery criteria should be explicit**

When do feature releases resume?

Not merely:

```text
new month started
```

Possible criteria:

```text
Known severe reliability defect fixed
        ↓
No critical burn-rate alert
        ↓
SLI trend stable
        ↓
High-priority remediation completed
        ↓
Product/SRE agreement
```

The exact policy varies, but there should be a clear exit condition.

---

# **16. Example**

Suppose:

```text
Checkout SLO = 99.95%
30-day window

Error budget = 0.05%
```

A new deployment consumes:

```text
80% of monthly budget
in 40 minutes
```

Response:

```text
1. Stop rollout
2. Roll back
3. Incident response
4. Determine why canary didn't catch it
5. Add SLO-based rollout gate
6. Fix defect
7. Test
8. Resume carefully
```

If the remaining 20% is later consumed:

```text
budget exhausted
```

I'd restrict discretionary risky releases until stability is restored.

---

### **Interview answer condensed**

> “When an error budget is exhausted, I would change the team's risk posture rather than blindly freeze everything. First I'd verify the SLI and understand what consumed the budget—whether it was one fast-burning incident, repeated deployments, or slow dependency degradation. I would pause or heavily gate nonessential risky releases and prioritize the reliability work responsible for restoring the SLO, such as fixing recurring failures, improving rollback, capacity, circuit breakers, or observability. Reliability fixes and urgent security changes can still ship. I'd use an error-budget policy agreed with engineering and product beforehand and define clear criteria for returning to normal release velocity.”

**Memory line:**
**An exhausted error budget means reliability temporarily takes priority over discretionary change velocity.**

**Interview trap:**
Error-budget exhaustion should **not mean a blind ban on every production change**, because reliability and security fixes may themselves require deployment.

**Keywords:**
Error budget, burn rate, SLO, change freeze, reliability work, release gating, postmortem, risk posture.

**Likely follow-ups:**

* Would you stop a critical security patch when the budget is exhausted?
* What's the difference between fast burn and slow burn?
* Who should define the error-budget policy?
* When should normal feature delivery resume?
* Would you lower the SLO if the team repeatedly fails to meet it?

# **T30-H06. How would you design an Azure workload to degrade gracefully when one dependency fails?**

**Answer:**

I would first classify dependencies into **critical and noncritical**, then define the exact degraded behavior for each failure before choosing technical patterns.

For a noncritical dependency, I would use bounded timeouts, carefully limited retries for transient faults, circuit breakers for persistent failures, bulkheads to prevent resource exhaustion, and fallbacks such as cached data, partial responses, asynchronous queues, or temporarily disabling a feature.

For example, if an e-commerce API depends on a recommendation service, the recommendation service failing should not take down checkout. The request should time out quickly, the circuit breaker should eventually stop sending traffic to the unhealthy dependency, and the application should return the product page without recommendations.

For business-critical dependencies such as payment authorization or inventory confirmation, I would **fail safely rather than invent a result**, potentially preserving the user's cart or queueing a workflow when business semantics allow it.

In Azure I could combine AKS/App Service, Azure Cache for Redis, Service Bus, API Management, health probes, Azure Monitor, and resiliency patterns such as circuit breaker and bulkhead. Microsoft specifically recommends circuit breakers to prevent repeated calls to failing dependencies and bulkheads to prevent one failure from cascading through shared resource pools. ([Microsoft Learn][7])

---

## **1. Start with dependency classification**

Imagine:

```text
Checkout API
│
├── Product Service
├── Inventory Service
├── Payment Provider
├── Recommendation Service
└── Email Notification
```

I would classify:

```text
Payment
→ critical to confirming payment

Inventory
→ critical before confirming stock

Recommendation
→ optional

Email
→ important but asynchronous

Product description enrichment
→ potentially optional
```

This tells me how each dependency is allowed to fail.

---

# **2. Define the degraded experience explicitly**

For each dependency:

```text
Dependency
      ↓
What happens if:
├── timeout?
├── 500?
├── 429?
├── unavailable for 30 sec?
└── unavailable for 2 hours?
```

Example:

| Dependency     | Failure response                   |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Recommendation | Hide recommendations               |
| Reviews        | Show product without reviews       |
| Email          | Queue notification                 |
| Payment        | Controlled checkout failure        |
| Inventory      | Don't confirm order until verified |

The application should know **before production** what the safe degradation mode is.

---

# **3. Put a timeout around every remote dependency**

Without timeout:

```text
Checkout API
     ↓
Recommendation API
     ↓
hangs for 60 seconds
     ↓
request threads/connections accumulate
     ↓
Checkout API exhausts resources
```

One dependency failure now becomes:

```text
whole-service outage
```

Better:

```text
Checkout request budget = 1 sec

Recommendation timeout = 150 ms
        ↓
timeout
        ↓
fallback immediately
```

The timeout should fit inside the overall user-request latency budget.

---

# **4. Retry only transient failures**

Suppose dependency responds with:

```text
temporary network reset
```

A small retry may succeed.

But:

```text
dependency is overloaded
```

plus aggressive retries can create:

```text
retry storm
```

Microsoft's current transient-fault guidance recommends finite retries, exponential backoff/jitter, and even aggregate retry budgets so many concurrent callers don't collectively overwhelm a recovering dependency. ([Microsoft Learn][8])

So:

```text
Transient error
     ↓
limited retry
     ↓
backoff + jitter
```

not:

```text
while(true):
    retry immediately
```

---

# **5. Add a circuit breaker**

Suppose the recommendation service repeatedly fails.

Circuit breaker:

```text
CLOSED
→ requests flow normally
```

Failures exceed threshold:

```text
OPEN
→ calls fail fast
→ fallback used
```

After cooldown:

```text
HALF-OPEN
→ allow a small number of probes
```

If healthy:

```text
CLOSED
```

If still failing:

```text
OPEN
```

Microsoft's current Circuit Breaker guidance explicitly says the pattern prevents repeated calls to a dependency likely to fail and can be used to trigger graceful degradation while the dependency recovers. ([Microsoft Learn][7])

---

# **6. Use bulkheads to stop cascading failure**

Suppose three downstream APIs use the same:

```text
thread pool
connection pool
```

Payment slows down.

Without isolation:

```text
Payment calls consume every worker
        ↓
Inventory can't execute
        ↓
Recommendation can't execute
        ↓
Checkout collapses
```

With bulkheads:

```text
Payment pool
Inventory pool
Recommendation pool
```

Payment can exhaust its own resources without consuming everything.

Microsoft's Bulkhead pattern specifically recommends isolated resource pools so one service or consumer failure doesn't cascade through the application. ([Microsoft Learn][9])

---

# **7. Use cache for safe read fallbacks**

Suppose:

```text
Catalog metadata service
```

temporarily fails.

If business rules permit:

```text
Azure Cache for Redis
       ↓
last known valid result
```

can provide degraded operation.

Flow:

```text
Call Product API
      ↓
timeout
      ↓
cache available?
  /              \
 Yes              No
 ↓                 ↓
serve stale       controlled
safe data         fallback
```

But I would set a freshness boundary.

For example:

```text
Product description
→ maybe safe for 1 hour

Bank balance
→ not safe stale

Inventory count
→ potentially unsafe stale
```

Graceful degradation must not sacrifice correctness.

---

# **8. Decouple noncritical work with Service Bus**

Suppose order creation performs:

```text
Save order
      ↓
Send confirmation email
```

synchronously.

If the mail provider fails:

```text
order request fails
```

even though the order itself could have succeeded.

Better:

```text
Order API
   ↓
Commit order
   ↓
Azure Service Bus
   ↓
Notification worker
   ↓
Email provider
```

If email is down:

```text
message remains/retries
```

while checkout continues.

This is both:

```text
graceful degradation
+
fault isolation
```

---

# **9. Queue-based load leveling also protects dependencies**

Suppose downstream ERP can process only:

```text
100 jobs/sec
```

but checkout produces:

```text
2,000 jobs/sec
```

Without queue:

```text
ERP overloaded
      ↓
timeouts
      ↓
retries
      ↓
bigger outage
```

With Service Bus:

```text
API
 ↓
Queue
 ↓
Workers consume
at controlled rate
 ↓
ERP
```

The queue absorbs the burst.

Microsoft's Azure Architecture Center recommends queue-based load leveling and asynchronous decoupling as part of self-healing/resilient cloud design. ([Microsoft Learn][10])

---

# **10. Rate limit and throttle at the edge**

Suppose one client creates excessive traffic.

```text
Client A
→ 50,000 req/sec
```

This can consume capacity required for every customer.

Using:

```text
Azure API Management
```

or appropriate ingress controls:

```text
Per-client throttling
       ↓
protect backend
```

can keep the core service available.

Bulkhead guidance specifically calls out built-in Azure controls such as API Management rate limits and AKS resource limits rather than reinventing isolation mechanisms unnecessarily. ([Microsoft Learn][9])

---

# **11. Separate critical and optional execution resources**

On AKS, for example:

```text
Critical checkout
→ dedicated resources/node pool where justified

Recommendation
→ lower-priority workload
```

At a minimum:

```text
resource requests
resource limits
ResourceQuota
PriorityClass
```

can help prevent optional workloads from consuming all cluster resources.

For stronger failure isolation:

```text
separate node pools
or
separate clusters
```

may be justified.

The boundary should match business criticality.

---

# **12. Health probes should reflect actual service readiness**

Suppose the API process is running, but:

```text
critical database connection unavailable
```

A simple probe:

```text
/process-is-running
```

may report healthy.

The readiness model should answer:

```text
Can this instance safely serve requests?
```

Load balancers/orchestrators can then stop sending traffic to instances that are unable to serve correctly.

Azure's self-healing guidance explicitly recommends health endpoints so load balancers and orchestrators can detect unhealthy service instances and route accordingly. ([Microsoft Learn][10])

---

# **13. Be careful with dependency-aware health probes**

However, don't make:

```text
recommendation service down
      ↓
checkout readiness = unhealthy
```

if recommendation is optional.

Otherwise:

```text
optional dependency failure
      ↓
every checkout Pod becomes NotReady
      ↓
entire application goes offline
```

which defeats graceful degradation.

Health checks should distinguish:

```text
critical dependency
vs
optional dependency
```

---

# **14. Example Azure architecture**

```text
                         Azure Front Door
                                ↓
                          API Management
                                ↓
                         AKS / App Service
                                ↓
                    ┌───────────┼────────────┐
                    │           │            │
                    ▼           ▼            ▼
                Azure SQL    Redis       Service Bus
                 critical    fallback     async work
                    │                         │
                    │                         ▼
                    │                   Notification
                    │                     Worker
                    │
                    └─────────────┐
                                  │
                           Core transaction

Optional Recommendation API
           ↑
           │
 Timeout + Retry
           │
 Circuit Breaker
           │
           ↓
If unavailable
→ omit feature
```

# **15. Failure sequence: recommendation service fails**

### Normal

```text
Request
  ↓
Checkout/Product API
  ↓
Recommendation API
  ↓
200
```

### Dependency starts failing

```text
Recommendation
→ timeout
```

Application performs:

```text
limited retry
```

Then:

```text
failure threshold reached
      ↓
Circuit opens
```

Future requests:

```text
do NOT wait for recommendation
        ↓
immediate fallback
        ↓
core response returned
```

This protects:

```text
latency
thread pool
connection pool
downstream service
```

# **16. Monitor degradation explicitly**

A degraded system can still return:

```text
HTTP 200
```

Therefore normal availability alerts may not fire.

I would emit metrics such as:

```text
recommendation_fallback_rate
circuit_breaker_open
dependency_timeout_rate
queue_depth
cache_fallback_rate
```

and monitor them through:

```text
Azure Monitor
Application Insights
Prometheus
```

For example:

```text
fallback rate > 20%
for 10 min
      ↓
alert
```

The customer experience may still be acceptable, but operators need to know the system is degraded.

---

# **17. Observe error-budget impact**

Suppose:

```text
Recommendation down
```

but checkout succeeds.

If recommendations are not part of the checkout SLI:

```text
checkout availability SLO
may remain healthy
```

That's appropriate.

However, recommendation may have its **own SLO**.

This prevents optional dependency failure from consuming the wrong service's reliability budget.

---

# **18. Avoid retry storms**

An important failure scenario:

```text
Dependency handles 1,000 RPS normally
       ↓
becomes unhealthy
       ↓
each caller retries 5×
       ↓
5,000+ attempts
       ↓
dependency can't recover
```

Microsoft explicitly identifies retry storms as a cloud antipattern and recommends circuit breakers, throttling, and honoring service retry guidance such as `Retry-After` where applicable. ([Microsoft Learn][11])

Retries should therefore be:

```text
bounded
backed off
jittered
aware of status/error semantics
```

---

# **19. Critical dependencies must fail safely**

Suppose payment authorization fails.

Wrong:

```text
Payment unavailable
      ↓
fallback:
assume paid
```

Correct:

```text
Payment unavailable
      ↓
preserve cart/order intent
      ↓
show controlled temporary error
or
queue only if business semantics permit
```

Graceful degradation does not mean:

```text
always return success
```

It means:

```text
preserve as much correct functionality
as possible
```

---

# **20. Test degraded modes intentionally**

Don't wait until production.

I would test:

```text
Recommendation returns 500
Recommendation times out
Redis unavailable
Service Bus throttled
Payment provider slow
Database failover
DNS failure
```

Then verify:

```text
Expected fallback?
Latency bounded?
No retry storm?
No resource exhaustion?
Alerts fire?
Core SLO protected?
```

Microsoft's mission-critical workload guidance explicitly recommends building degradation boundaries into the design and using queues, limited retries, and circuit breakers to prevent downstream failures from overwhelming the system. ([Microsoft Learn][12])

---

# **21. Recovery behavior matters too**

When dependency recovers:

```text
Circuit breaker
      ↓
Half-open
      ↓
few test requests
```

If healthy:

```text
gradually resume normal traffic
```

I don't want:

```text
dependency returns
      ↓
all callers immediately flood it
      ↓
dependency crashes again
```

Recovery should be controlled.

---

### **Interview answer condensed**

> “I would first classify dependencies as critical or optional and define a safe degraded behavior for each. Remote calls would have bounded timeouts, limited exponential-backoff retries for transient failures, and circuit breakers for persistent failures. I would use bulkheads so one dependency can't exhaust all threads or connections, cache safe read data where stale values are acceptable, and use Service Bus to decouple noncritical asynchronous work. For example, if recommendations fail, checkout should simply omit recommendations; if payment authorization fails, I would fail safely rather than invent a successful result. I would also monitor fallback rate and circuit state because an application can return HTTP 200 while operating in a degraded mode, and I'd regularly test those failure scenarios.”

**Memory line:**
**Graceful degradation means isolate the failed dependency, fail fast, use a safe fallback, and preserve the core business function.**

**Interview trap:**
An optional dependency must **not be included blindly in a readiness check** if making it unhealthy would cause every otherwise-functional application instance to be removed from traffic.

Graceful degradation, timeout, retry, exponential backoff, circuit breaker, bulkhead, Azure Service Bus, Redis, fallback, fault isolation, retry storm.
