### **T12-E02. What is disaster recovery?**

**Answer:**
Disaster recovery, or **DR**, is the strategy and process for restoring an application or service after a **major failure**, such as an Azure region outage, large-scale infrastructure failure, ransomware event, or other disaster that makes the primary environment unavailable.

The goal is not only to recover data, but to restore the **entire business service within agreed RTO and RPO targets**.

### **Simple architecture**

```text
Primary Region
     │
     ├── Application
     ├── Database
     └── Storage
           │
           │ replication / backup
           ↓
Secondary Region
     │
     ├── Standby Application
     ├── Replicated Data
     └── Recovery Infrastructure
```

If the primary region fails:

```text
Primary unavailable
       ↓
DR failover
       ↓
Secondary region becomes active
       ↓
Users redirected
```

### **Production example**

Suppose an application runs in:

```text
East US
```

and its DR environment is in:

```text
West US
```

The application could use:

```text
Front Door / Traffic Manager
         ↓
Primary region
         +
Secondary region
```

while the database uses an appropriate cross-region replication mechanism.

During a regional failure:

```text
Detect outage
    ↓
Fail over data/service
    ↓
Redirect traffic
    ↓
Validate application
```

### **Important distinction**

```text
High Availability
→ handles failures while trying to keep service continuously available

Disaster Recovery
→ restores service after a major failure/disaster
```

For example:

```text
One VM fails
→ HA

Entire region fails
→ DR / multi-region design
```

**Memory line:**
**DR is how you recover the business service after a major outage.**

**Interview trap:**
Do not describe DR as just “taking backups.” Backup protects data; DR covers **restoring the complete service**, including compute, networking, application configuration, data, DNS/traffic routing, and operational procedures.

**Keywords:**
Disaster recovery, DR, failover, secondary region, RTO, RPO, business continuity

### **T12-E03. What is RTO?**

**Answer:**
RTO stands for **Recovery Time Objective**. It is the **maximum acceptable amount of time a service can remain unavailable after a disaster before it must be restored**.

In simple terms:

> **RTO answers: “How quickly must we recover?”**

### **Example**

Suppose:

```text
RTO = 30 minutes
```

and the application fails at:

```text
10:00 AM
```

Then the business expects the service to be restored by approximately:

```text
10:30 AM
```

Conceptually:

```text
Failure
10:00
  │
  │------ maximum allowed downtime ------│
                                         ↓
                                       10:30
                                      RTO limit
```

### **Architecture impact**

A very low RTO may require:

```text
Pre-provisioned secondary infrastructure
Automatic failover
Cross-region replication
Automated DNS/traffic switching
Regular DR testing
```

Whereas:

```text
RTO = 24 hours
```

might allow infrastructure to be recreated from Terraform and backups after the disaster.

Generally:

```text
Lower RTO
→ faster recovery
→ more automation/redundancy
→ higher cost
```

### **Important distinction**

```text
RTO
→ downtime tolerance

RPO
→ data-loss tolerance
```

**Memory line:**
**RTO = how long can the service be down?**

**Interview trap:**
Do not confuse RTO with backup frequency. Backup frequency is more closely related to **RPO**, not RTO.

**Keywords:**
RTO, recovery time, downtime, failover, business requirement

### **T12-E04. What is RPO?**

**Answer:**
RPO stands for **Recovery Point Objective**. It defines the **maximum acceptable amount of data loss measured in time** after a disaster.

In simple terms:

> **RPO answers: “How much recent data can we afford to lose?”**

### **Example**

Suppose:

```text
RPO = 5 minutes
```

and a disaster happens at:

```text
10:00 AM
```

The recovered system should ideally contain data at least up to:

```text
9:55 AM
```

So the maximum acceptable data loss is:

```text
5 minutes
```

Conceptually:

```text
Last recoverable point      Disaster
       09:55                  10:00
         │----------------------│
                 5 min
                  RPO
```

### **Architecture impact**

If:

```text
RPO = 24 hours
```

a daily backup might satisfy the business requirement.

But if:

```text
RPO = 5 minutes
```

daily backup definitely will not be enough.

You may need:

```text
Continuous / frequent replication
Database geo-replication
Transaction-log replication
Storage replication
```

depending on the service.

If:

```text
RPO ≈ 0
```

the architecture needs very strong synchronous or near-real-time data replication, which usually increases complexity and cost.

### **RTO vs RPO**

Remember:

```text
RTO
→ How quickly must I recover?

RPO
→ How much data may I lose?
```

Example:

```text
RTO = 30 minutes
RPO = 5 minutes
```

means:

```text
Restore the application within 30 minutes
AND
Lose no more than about 5 minutes of data
```

**Memory line:**
**RPO = how far back in time can my recovered data be?**

**Interview trap:**
RPO does **not** mean how long recovery takes. That is RTO.

**Keywords:**
RPO, data loss, recovery point, replication, backup frequency

### **T12-E05. What is Azure Backup?**

**Answer:**
Azure Backup is Azure's managed **data-protection and backup service** used to create and manage recovery points for supported Azure and hybrid workloads. It helps protect data against accidental deletion, corruption, operational mistakes, and other data-loss scenarios.

### **How it works conceptually**

```text
Production workload
       ↓
Azure Backup policy
       ↓
Scheduled backup
       ↓
Recovery points
       ↓
Vault
       ↓
Restore when required
```

Depending on the workload and backup architecture, Azure Backup works with Azure backup vault technologies such as a **Recovery Services vault** or **Backup vault**.

### **What a backup policy controls**

A policy typically defines:

```text
When should backups run?
        +
How long should recovery points be retained?
```

For example:

```text
Daily backup
     ↓
Keep daily points for 30 days
     ↓
Keep monthly points for 12 months
```

### **Production example**

Suppose a VM contains important application data.

If someone accidentally deletes files:

```text
Files deleted
     ↓
Select valid recovery point
     ↓
Azure Backup restore
     ↓
Recover files / workload
```

Azure Backup can protect supported resources such as VMs and several other Azure workload types, although the exact backup mechanism varies by service.

### **Backup is different from HA**

```text
High Availability
→ keeps application running

Backup
→ gives historical recovery points
```

Having two running replicas does not protect you from every logical error.

For example:

```text
Bad DELETE executed
       ↓
Primary data deleted
       ↓
Replication copies deletion
```

A historical backup may still allow recovery to a point before the deletion.

### **Backup is also different from DR**

```text
Azure Backup
→ recover data/workload from recovery points

Disaster Recovery
→ restore the complete service after major failure
```

They often work together.

**Memory line:**
**Azure Backup provides managed recovery points so data and supported workloads can be restored after loss or corruption.**

**Interview trap:**
Replication is **not a replacement for backup**. Replication may replicate accidental deletion or corruption to the secondary copy.

**Keywords:**
Azure Backup, recovery point, backup policy, retention, restore, Recovery Services vault, data protection
